The bench was quiet except for the small fan in the power supply and the soft click of a keyboard. A short cable ran from the board to the host, and the only job for the next hour was to prove that bytes written on one side came back unchanged on the other. The operator opened the port, set the rate, and watched the status line confirm that the link was up. Then a long paragraph was typed into the box and sent as one burst, because a single sentence never stresses a buffer the way a page of ordinary English does.

Morning light came through the window and lay across the notes. The first page listed the port name, the chosen rate, the number of data bits, and the rule that each character should survive the trip without a dropped bit or an added gap that the reader could not explain. The second page was blank on purpose. It would be filled only after the echo had been compared, line by line, with the text that left the host. Nobody wanted a guess. The test was finished only when the two copies matched.

A river of words is a better load than a repeating letter. Repeating letters can hide a stuck bit, while real sentences mix short words with long ones and place commas, periods, and question marks where a person would expect them. Numbers appear when a count is needed. Capital letters appear at the start of a sentence. Spaces separate tokens so that a later check can split the stream and count the words. If the count on the way back is the same as the count on the way out, the frame of the test is already healthier than a glance at the screen can show.

The room held a second chair for a colleague who liked to read the returning text aloud. Hearing the sentences made skips obvious. A missing the, a doubled and, or a word cut in half at the end of a line was easier to catch by ear than by eye after the first few hundred words. They agreed to read only after the whole block had arrived, so that a pause in speech would not be confused with a pause on the wire. The wire had its own timing. Speech could wait.

Outside, traffic moved along the street in uneven groups. Inside, the test stayed still. The board did not need music for this check, and the host did not need a graph. It needed a block of language long enough to cross more than one buffer and ordinary enough that any damage would look like damage. A poem with odd line breaks might be mistaken for formatting. A table might be mistaken for alignment. Plain prose leaves fewer excuses.

The story they chose was about a walk that never happened. A person left a small flat at dawn, locked the door, and turned toward a park that was six blocks away. The air was cold enough to show breath and warm enough that gloves stayed in a pocket. A bakery was already open. The smell of bread reached the corner before the sign could be read. The walker bought nothing, because the point of the walk was the distance, not the food. At the park gate a dog waited for its owner and watched the path with patient attention. Children were not there yet. The grass held a thin shine of dew. A bench faced the pond, and the pond held a few birds that did not care about serial ports, baud rates, or the careful counting of words.

The walker sat and listed the things that could fail on a link even when the text looked fine at a glance. A byte could be lost at the start, so the first word would arrive late or not at all. A byte could be lost at the end, so the last word would be short. A byte could be changed in the middle, so a common word would become a strange one. Two bytes could be swapped if some stage stored them in the wrong order. A long pause could be inserted if a buffer filled and the sender waited. None of these faults is dramatic in a single word. All of them become visible when the passage is long, familiar, and checked with patience rather than with hope.

By the time the imagined walker stood up, the sun had cleared the trees. The path back passed the same bakery, now with a short line of people who had come for the same smell. The dog had gone. The bench was empty. The flat was where it had been left, which is the least exciting and most useful result a test can have. The key turned. The door opened. The notes were still on the table, and the second page was still blank until the comparison was done.

They printed both copies, though printing was not required. Paper makes a slow reader honest. A finger can rest under a line, move to the next, and refuse to skip. On a screen the eye jumps. On paper the eye walks. The first hundred words were dull on purpose, full of the, and, and of, because those words are easy to overlook and therefore good traps. The middle of the passage carried the walk, the park, and the list of faults, so that a reader who grew bored would still have landmarks. The last stretch returned to the bench and the cable and the simple claim that the text which returns should be the text which was sent.

A clock on the wall moved while they read. It did not measure the bits. It measured how long two people were willing to look before they said the link was good. The numbers agreed. The paragraphs agreed. The punctuation agreed. The link had carried a thousand words and brought each one back.
